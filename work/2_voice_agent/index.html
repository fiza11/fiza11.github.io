What actually improved a speech-to-speech tutor

Five architectures, a fine-tuning programme on Qwen3-Omni, and the finding that the largest gains came from outside the model.

Between February and September 2026 I led the research behind Stimuler’s speech-to-speech tutor, an always-available voice partner for English learners calling from noisy phones. It had to do three things at once: understand heavily accented speech, reply fast enough to feel like a conversation, and sound like someone a learner would want to keep talking to. Every off-the-shelf model I tried failed at least one of them.

This page covers the comparison that picked the architecture, what fine-tuning bought, and the result that surprised me most: once the system was live, the biggest quality gains came from fixing the pipeline around the model, not the model.

Five architectures

I built or benchmarked five approaches before committing to one.

In more detail:

Approach What I tested Outcome
Full-duplex Moshi-style model (7B) LoRA (r=128) on 1,154 hours of real learner calls Rejected. The voice sounded robotic because of the 1.1 kbps Mimi codec, which no adapter can fix. Bolting on a separate TTS “mouth” broke duplex timing.
Cascade: Whisper Large v3 → Llama 3.2 3B + LoRA → 3B TTS Seven duplex control tokens (user is interrupting, user backchannel, …) learned from 50K dialogues split into micro-turns, with loss up-weighted on turn boundaries Worked as a proof of concept. Compute latency fell from 2.82s to 0.80s, but about 2.5s as heard, and three models to maintain.
NVIDIA Nemotron VoiceChat (12B) Benchmarked on 325 DailyTalk samples Best conversational style, but its duplex loop treated background noise, mic hum and its own output as user speech. Closed container, so it can’t be fine-tuned.
Covo-Audio, MiniCPM-o-4.5 One-day spikes Below the quality bar.
Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B (MoE, 3B active), base + system prompt Served with vLLM-Omni Shipped, at under 600ms to first audio.

On the DailyTalk benchmark, the base Qwen3-Omni model talked like an assistant rather than a conversation partner: long replies that almost always asked a question. Fine-tuning fixed the style, at a small cost in intelligibility.

Metric (325 DailyTalk samples) Qwen3-Omni base Qwen3-Omni fine-tuned Nemotron VoiceChat
Words per response 19.8 5.8 10.7
Question rate 98.8% 9.2% 11.4%
WER of the spoken reply 7.63% 9.83% 7.86%
Style distance to human dialogue (↓) 0.180 0.115 0.099

What fine-tuning bought

I adapted only the Thinker, the part of Qwen3-Omni that decides what to say, and left the Talker and the audio decoder frozen. Over seven rounds of data (4,693 to 13,877 examples) and a rank sweep at 8, 16 and 32, the best adapter improved an LLM judge’s overall score from 2.78 to 3.14, warmth from 3.74 to 3.95, and WER of the spoken output from 3.5% to 2.1%.

Where the gains actually came from

After launch, the largest improvement came from finding bugs in the pipeline that fed the model, rather than from anything inside it. Together these fixes cut severe comprehension failures by about two thirds.

  • The model was reading its own words. Our first prompt A/B test (n=2,250) showed two very different prompts performing identically. That should not happen, so instead of picking a winner I went looking for why. Past learner turns were being stored as a placeholder that echoed the tutor’s previous reply, so the model was rebuilding the conversation from its own side only. Fixing it halved severe comprehension failures on its own.
  • More context helped, up to a point. Doubling history from 4 to 8 turns helped. Doubling again to 16 did nothing: about 95% of long-call failures happen in the moment, not because something was forgotten. That falsified the “long calls need more memory” hypothesis, so we reverted.
  • Interruptions evicted history. About half of all turns are interrupted, and a turn store capped at 12 entries was silently evicting real context.
  • Connections reset mid-call. About 5% of calls reconnected with an empty history. I traced it to a WebSocket library’s 16-frame buffer stalling the keep-alive after a learner barged in. It never appeared in our analytics tables.
  • Background noise kept turns open. Noise held the voice-activity detector above its deactivation threshold, so the learner’s turn never closed and the call stalled. Adding noise cancellation in front of it raised real-speech turn commits from 49.7% to 89.3%.

How I measured it

Speech-to-speech has no reference transcript, so word error rate doesn’t apply, and latency is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. I scored three axes that fail independently: did it understand, was the reply worth making, and does it sound like someone you want to keep talking to. I kept them separate rather than blending them into one score.

The rule that saved me most often was a hierarchy of trust. A metric decides only if it is a voluntary, costly user action that our own instruments can’t change underneath it, such as whether a learner comes back. Judge scores explain why something changed and never decide. Twice an apparent win failed that test. A memory feature looked like it added 100 seconds of talk time, and a rolling summary looked like it raised return rates by 14 points. Both were selection bias: the users the feature fired for were already the engaged ones. A quantised model went the other way. It was 9–11% faster to first audio, but learners came back measurably less, so I turned it off.

How the judge itself was validated is on its own page, and the reasoning behind the three axes is in How do you evaluate a conversation?.

What I would flag

The architecture comparison was shaped by our constraints, so a team without the latency budget or accent mix we had might rank Nemotron or the cascade differently. And the pipeline fixes were measured as before-and-after comparisons on live traffic, with matched cohorts where possible but not always as randomised experiments.
